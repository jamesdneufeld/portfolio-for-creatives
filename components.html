Portfolios Are Designed Systems

Components can be added to any page. Once you create a section you like, you can reuse it elsewhere instead of rebuilding it from scratch.

Figma’s 2025 Shifting Roles report explores how the boundaries between design and development are becoming less distinct. Understanding HTML and CSS helps designers see how their work behaves beyond the original composition, giving them more control over how it is built and adapted.

Your portfolio is a designed system, not just a finished composition. Reusable components make your site easier to build, maintain, update, and adapt as your work changes. A hand-coded portfolio gives you a durable foundation and a free option you can continue developing long after you graduate.

Full-Width Text Block

Use this for key statements or case study introductions. On larger screens, the text is limited to 70% width to keep line lengths comfortable and give the content more breathing room. The section minimum height is set to 80vh, with the text area at 60vh. Both the width and height values can be changed in style.css , and the layout expands to 100% width on smaller screens.

Content-Height Text Block

Use this for key statements or case study introductions when you want the section to fit its content rather than fill most of the screen. The section has no minimum height, so its height is determined by the text and any padding applied in style.css . The text width is limited to 70% on larger screens and expands to 100% on smaller screens.

Optional JavaScript Effects

Add playful interactions and small surprises to make your portfolio more engaging and memorable.

Random Image on Hover

An image that randomly changes when you hover over it. Inspired by IDEA grad Noah Kawamura , who incorporated this interaction on his grad portfolio. Other students have since adapted it in different ways, including Valentina Kruglikovskaya , who used it to show multiple versions of her logo. A simple way to make images, projects, or other work more playful and interactive, rewarding visitors who take the time to explore. Read Your Portfolio is a Secret Garden .

Phrase Rotator

Emma is

A small JavaScript effect inspired by Emma Sato , whose portfolio used a rotating line of phrases in the footer to hide little pieces of personality and story throughout her site. One of her phrases, “preaching to the choir,” led to a video of her singing as a child. Nice work, Emma! Each phrase can link to a different website, image, video, or other URL, turning an ordinary part of a portfolio into something visitors can discover. The best portfolio interactions don't have to announce themselves. A tiny piece of JavaScript, a strange link, an unexpected animation, or a hidden page can reward visitors who take the time to explore. Read Your Portfolio is a Secret Garden .

Random Text on Refresh

This text changes each time you refresh the page.

A tiny JavaScript effect inspired by Elizabeth Lin , whose portfolio served up a random pun every time you refreshed the page. There’s nothing particularly complicated happening here, which is part of the fun. A small surprise like this can give a portfolio some personality and reward visitors who take the time to look around, and make a site feel fresh rather than static or stale. Read Your Portfolio is a Secret Garden .

Scroll Reveal

A heading can reveal itself.

So can a paragraph, image, project card, or almost anything else.

A simple scroll-based reveal effect that fades and moves elements into place as they enter the viewport. JavaScript watches for elements entering the viewport and adds a class, while CSS controls the animation. Add the reveal class to headings, images, project cards, or other elements you want to animate. The effect also respects visitors who prefer reduced motion. Read Your Portfolio is a Secret Garden .

Want more JavaScript effects?

This Components page is updated regularly. If you have an idea for a JavaScript effect, even if you're not sure how to build it, ask James. Not every effect ages well. Typewriter text was novel in 2015 and now reads as a portfolio trope, especially on hero headlines. Parallax is similar. Both can still work when they actually fit the project. Trends become tropes when they are used for decoration instead of communication. A useful test is: “If I remove this effect, does the website lose meaning, or just flash?” If it loses meaning, the effect is doing its job. If it just loses flash, it is probably a trope.

For more ideas, browse James' Pens on CodePen . The Pens tab and the Loved tab are both worth a look. Codrops is worth reading because the write-ups explain how the effects work. Free Frontend is a gallery, good for finding things, but less useful for understanding them. Both lean toward effects that look great in a demo, which is exactly the trap. Browse them for technique, not things to paste in.
